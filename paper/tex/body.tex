\section{Results}

\subsection{One crystal, one row, across 55 databases}
\label{sec:index}

Making 55 databases comparable comes down to one decision, taken once per
incoming record: is this crystal already in the corpus? We settle it on the
structure rather than on the label. Each record is matched first on a
cross-database identifier the source is entitled to assert, then on a structural
fingerprint, and never on the chemical formula, which polymorphs share
(Fig.~\ref{fig:record}a; Methods). CaMg$_2$Sb$_2$, the crystal drawn there,
ends the process as one row carrying 21 references from 17 of the 55 databases,
no two of which name it the same way. A record that matches nothing is
admitted rather than discarded, and every value it carries keeps the source, the
native record and the method behind it. That second choice has a consequence this
section measures: because an admitted record joins the criterion applied to the
next, what results is not a merge of 55 databases but a corpus that extends the
substrate it recognises them against.

The corpus so assembled holds 14{,}590{,}485 distinct crystal structures, of
which 357{,}127 are experimental and 14{,}233{,}358 are computational candidates.
On that pool sit 131{,}824{,}881 property values spanning 166 properties,
1{,}198{,}897 stored spectra and 21{,}543{,}633 references recording which
database contains which crystal.

The contributors are extremely unequal, spanning 5.7 orders of magnitude, with
the largest supplying 46.0\% of all values and 30 of the 55 supplying fewer than
$10^5$ each (Fig.~\ref{fig:record}b). The two ends of that distribution do
different work: the large general-purpose databases determine how much of the
pool carries any property at all, while the small specialised libraries determine
how many distinct properties exist to carry
\cite{haastrup2018c2db,zhou20192dmatpedia,zhang2019catalogue} \textemdash{} the
most property-diverse contributor supplies 61 of the 166 properties, the largest
supplies 10.

The corpus is considerably larger than the pool it started from, and the
difference is exactly the records that matched nothing. The deduplicated starting
pool held 8{,}225{,}138 structures, and admission added 6{,}365{,}347 more, a
growth of 77.4\%.

What that union is made of differs completely from one database to the next, and
the difference is invisible from inside any of them
(Fig.~\ref{fig:record}c). Of the 5{,}894{,}222 crystals Alexandria annotates,
75.4\% are annotated by no other database; of the 211{,}854 the Materials Project
annotates, 1.3\% are. The enumerated framework libraries sit at 100\%,
describing crystals nobody else has computed, while several specialised libraries
sit below 1\%, reporting only on crystals everybody already holds. A campaign
that loads one database is therefore not working with a smaller copy of the
record but with a differently shaped piece of it, and no query inside that
database can reveal which.

Of the
21{,}543{,}633 references, 6{,}784{,}564 were resolved by record identifier,
8{,}294{,}560 by structural fingerprint alone, and the remainder record a
database's own entry (Fig.~\ref{fig:record}d). The two matched routes are
not interchangeable: identifier matching is exact but available only where a
database publishes a cross-reference it owns, while fingerprint matching is
available everywhere but has a domain of validity.

That domain has an edge, and it is worth stating before any conclusion is drawn
from the corpus. Enumerated framework libraries are generated by decorating a
small number of parent topologies, so sibling entries are identical in everything
a bonding-graph fingerprint reads and more than $10^5$ distinct hypothetical
frameworks would collapse onto a few hundred pool materials. Such libraries are
detected at the level of the source rather than the record and admitted under a
composite key, and the boundary of validity this establishes is stated in
Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:fingerprint}.

A structure admitted from one database becomes recognisable to every database
that follows, because its fingerprint and identifiers join the matching substrate
as it is admitted. The corpus therefore does not merely accumulate: the criterion
by which it recognises the next record is itself a product of the records already
ingested, and it is by this mechanism that the database keeps itself current as
sources publish new versions and new sources appear. The effect is measurable. Of
the 15{,}079{,}124 references resolved by matching, 3{,}676{,}707, or 24.4\%,
land on a crystal that was absent from the starting pool and was admitted during
assembly (Fig.~\ref{fig:record}e). Those links do not exist in a construction
that matches every database against a fixed reference. The figure is a property
of the assembly rather than of the sources, since a different ingestion order
would distribute it differently between them, and it is a lower bound on what a
frozen reference would have missed rather than a measure of quality. What it
establishes is that such links exist at all, and how much of the record they
reach.

The effect is ordered by ingestion time and by chemical distance from the
starting pool. AFLOW, ingested shortly after Alexandria had contributed
1{,}514{,}488 structures, resolved 47.7\% of its 1{,}401{,}895 matches onto
crystals admitted during assembly; the enumerated framework libraries show it in
its purest form, CoRE MOF resolving 99.5\% of its matches onto frameworks four
earlier libraries had placed in the pool; and databases whose chemistry the
starting pool already covered sit at or near zero. The per-source figures are
Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:order}. The practical consequence is that the
marginal value of adding a database is not confined to the values it brings. CoRE
MOF contributed 155{,}488 experimental porosity measurements, and the reason they
landed on existing crystals rather than forming an isolated set of new ones is
that earlier libraries had already put those frameworks in the pool. Assembled
against a frozen reference the same measurements would have arrived as an
unconnected island, and the corpus would have to be rebuilt from the beginning to
connect them.

What the assembled record holds is therefore not a uniform gain but a long tail.
Of the 13{,}902{,}836 structures carrying at least one reference, 3{,}233{,}769
carry two or more and a few hundred carry the account of twenty databases or
more, while the single largest group is described by one database and no other
(Fig.~\ref{fig:record}f). That distribution is what the next section measures
against: it is both the only thing that makes a cross-database comparison
possible and the reason a screen loading one database cannot see what it is
missing.

\subsection{What a forward screen inherits}
\label{sec:inherits}

A forward screen inherits two things from whichever database it loads: the labels
it trains on and the pool it ranks. Neither can be measured from inside that
database, because each is a property of the record as a whole, and measured on the
resolved union both turn out to be worse than the loaded database is able to
report. Where two independent sources describe the same crystal they disagree by
more than the margin conventionally used to decide whether a compound is stable,
and almost a quarter of the labelled record is unavailable from any single
provider. This section establishes the two, and they are the reason the
completion of Section~\ref{sec:completion} has to be run over the whole record
rather than over a slice of it.

The labels first. The redundancy just described is what makes a comparison
possible at all, and it is concentrated: the largest single overlap between two
databases holds 1{,}672{,}167 shared crystals (Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}a). That
redundancy and it also bounds what any
cross-database evaluation can claim, since for the largest overlapping pairs a
model trained on one repository and tested on another is tested on hundreds of
thousands of crystals it has already seen unless the split is taken over
structures. The distribution of that redundancy over databases and over degree is
Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:agreement}.

Comparing the redundant copies is possible only because every value kept the
method that produced it. Each method string is collapsed to a level of theory and
two databases are held against one another only where they answer the same
question, so that a pair meeting only across that boundary is not compared at all
(Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}b--d; Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:agreement}).
On that basis 846{,}303 crystals carry a formation energy from two or more
databases at one level of theory and 1{,}360{,}573 carry a band gap. Concordance
falls as the quantity becomes harder to compute: within 50~meV\,atom$^{-1}$,
65.3\% of formation energies and 64.2\% of hull distances agree, within 50~meV
82.1\% of band gaps do, and within 5~GPa 44.3\% of bulk moduli do. The tail
matters more than these medians. The tenth of shared formation energies that
agree least differ by more than 205~meV\,atom$^{-1}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}c), several times the hull-distance margins
conventionally used to call a compound stable
\cite{sun2016thermodynamic,bartel2020critical}. Whether a candidate passes such a
filter can therefore depend on which database supplied its energy, and in a
corpus without structural identity that dependence is not merely unquantified but
unobservable.

Three features of the comparison sharpen that result rather than softening it,
and each is established in Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:agreement}. Agreement
between two databases is not evidence of independence: the pairs that agree most
closely are those carrying one underlying calculation twice, so the concordant
fractions above overstate the true level of corroboration. The disagreement is
not a failure of the underlying theory, which independent implementations
reproduce far more closely than this \cite{lejaeghere2016reproducibility}, but of
the choices each provider makes around the solver, so it will not be removed by
better software; for the major pairs it appears as an offset of up to
177~meV\,atom$^{-1}$ carrying one sign, with correlations above 0.85. And it is
not an artefact of comparing different quantities, since pairs that meet only
across a boundary of theory are excluded, which lowers the measured disagreement
rather than raising it.

The pool is the second inheritance, and the question it raises is how much of it
carries any label at all. The answer is: very little, and what there is was
distributed neither by importance nor by cost. Of the 166 properties in the
registry 151 carry at least one value, and treating the pool and those 151 as a
matrix gives 2{,}203{,}163{,}235 cells of which 99{,}493{,}466 are occupied, so
the matrix is 4.52\% full (Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}e). Occupancy is extremely
uneven: nine properties cover more than 40\% of the pool while 97 of the 151
cover less than 1\%, and half of all crystals carry at most seven properties
(Supplementary Fig.~\ref{si-fig:contents}a). Only 66 of the 151 are reported by two or more
databases; the other 85 have a single supplier and therefore no independent check
of any kind.

The gradient follows price, and then departs from it in a way that identifies
what it really follows (Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}f). Of 69 general-purpose
properties, the 19 that are read off a self-consistent calculation a database
performs anyway have a median coverage of 4.56\% of the pool, while the 50 that
require a calculation of their own have a median of 0.107\%, lower by a factor of
43, and \emph{not one of the fifty reaches 10\%}. Within that second group,
however, the linear-response properties are the more expensive and reach a higher
median coverage (1.06\%) than those obtainable from a few extra self-consistent
runs (0.029\%), a thirty-six-fold inversion of what price predicts. The elastic
and dielectric tensors were the target of dedicated high-throughput campaigns
\cite{dejong2015charting,petousis2017high}; quantities such as the work function,
cheaper to obtain, were the target of none. What the coverage gradient records is
therefore not the difficulty of a calculation but whether somebody once organised
a campaign to perform it, and an expensive property that no campaign addressed is
not evidence that the property is unimportant, only that its turn has not come.
That map is legible only once the databases sit on one structure pool, because no
single repository can report on effort it did not spend.

Sparse as the matrix is, part of it was filled by the integration itself, which
is the section's second result (Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}g). Every structure has
exactly one contributor, the database that introduced it, and of the 6{,}266{,}517
structures admitted during assembly that carry any value, 1{,}637{,}460, or
26.1\%, carry at least one value their own contributor never reported. The effect
is largest where the chemistry is crowded, two thirds of the structures AFLOW
contributed having gained a value it never published, and close to zero for the
enumerated libraries of hypothetical frameworks, whose structures nobody else has
computed. Because a database's own structures are by construction those no
earlier source held, this figure depends on ingestion order; the order-independent
statement is that 3{,}153{,}748 crystals, 22.9\% of all that carry any value,
draw their property record from more than one database. Either way, almost a
quarter of the labelled record is not available from any single provider, so a
campaign that loads one database ranks a smaller pool than the one that exists
and has no indication of what it is missing. The two inheritances bound a screen
from opposite directions: the pool fixes what it can be asked about, and the
labels fix how far its answer can be trusted.

\subsection{Probing the limit of forward screening}
\label{sec:completion}

Both bounds of Section~\ref{sec:inherits} move together if the property layer is
completed by prediction over the whole record rather than over one provider's
slice of it. Whether such a completion succeeds is a question about trained
models, and it is answered when their held-out numbers exist. What can be
established now is that the problem is well posed on this corpus and is not well
posed on any single database, and that is what this section does.

The model that consumes it is built for that record rather than for a benchmark
(Fig.~\ref{fig:model}a). Each crystal enters as a periodic radius graph with
lattice self-edges, and every interaction block does three things in turn: an
attention over each edge taken in the frame of that edge, so that rotations are
handled by construction rather than by augmentation; a tensor square of the node
state, which lets one pass build the angular products a message-passing layer
would otherwise need several passes to reach; and a reciprocal-space term, in
which every atom emits a learned form factor and receives the origin-free Fourier
sum of the others over a fixed set of Miller indices, coupling every pair in the
cell and every periodic image at once rather than only the neighbours inside the
cutoff. The crystal is read out twice, once as the diffraction intensities those
form factors imply and once by attention over the atoms, and the head emits a
value, an uncertainty and the domain the value is valid on. The reciprocal terms
and the diffraction readout enter through zero-initialised gates, so the model at
initialisation is the real-space model and the extra machinery has to earn its
weight.

A completion needs three things no single database provides: structures
deduplicated, so that a train--test split falls on distinct crystals rather than
on records; labels that keep the quantity they denote rather than the column they
were filed under; and, beside each label, the spread of the sources that reported
it, which fixes the resolution at which a predicted value can be read, since a
prediction closer to the truth than the sources are to each other cannot be shown
to be so. The corpus supplies all three, and imposes four constraints on how they
may be used. Filling the same gap by calculation would take an effort no single
group can supply.

A census establishes the first half of that claim, which is that there is enough
to train on (Fig.~\ref{fig:completion}a). The unit that matters is not the
property but the
property at one level of theory, because a name such as band gap covers several
different physical quantities that a model cannot distinguish from structure
alone. Split that way, the 151 populated properties yield 513 candidate tasks,
set against the tens that existing property-prediction and stability benchmarks
provide \cite{dunn2020benchmarking,riebesell2025framework}. Of
these, 165 regression tasks have at least $10^4$ crystals to train on, 100 have at
least $10^5$, and 13 have at least $10^6$. Constructing the training sets under a
default policy that keeps the largest level of theory per property produced 176
sets covering 116 properties and 93{,}864{,}594 labels, each with a
chemistry-aware train, validation and test split.

The four constraints are the second half, and each is a direct consequence of
the integration reported above. The first is that a property name is not a label
definition
(Supplementary Fig.~\ref{si-fig:theory}).
Band gap carries 15 distinct levels of theory, of which the semilocal group holds
83\% and no other reaches 5\%. The systematic offsets between them
are of order 1~eV, which a model cannot infer from the structure and would
instead learn as a proxy for which database supplied the row. The second is that
some quantities are condition-dependent rather than multiply determined: carbon
dioxide uptake is reported at several distinct grand-canonical conditions, so a
material legitimately carries several different correct values and taking their
median produces a number that is not any physical quantity. Neither the level of
theory nor the condition is resolved away, and neither is the cross-source spread
of Section~\ref{sec:inherits}: across the training sets, 5{,}856{,}861 crystals
carry labels from two or more databases, and that spread is kept beside the
resolved value so that it can weight the loss rather than being averaged into
it.

The third is that the data cannot be split at random. The same compound
recurs in the pool under different accession numbers, so a random split places
identical chemistry in training and test, and the resulting scores are optimistic
in exactly the regime that matters, since the crystals a completion is wanted for
are the ones whose chemistry the training set never saw. Holding out whole
chemical systems is the right default, but it degenerates on the enumerated
libraries, where an entire task can occupy a single chemical system and a
chemistry-level holdout leaves the validation and test folds empty. The split
rule falls back for those tasks and records which level it used
(Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:census}).

The fourth is that the completion has a domain (Fig.~\ref{fig:completion}b,c).
Carbon dioxide uptake is a grand-canonical adsorption quantity: it is not zero for a
dense intermetallic, it is undefined, because the simulation cell has nowhere to
insert a molecule. Of the 176 training sets, 63 draw more than 90\% of their
labels from a single material family (Fig.~\ref{fig:completion}b). Enforcing
their declared domains changes the size of the completion problem substantially: 109 of
the 176 tasks are defined on less than half the pool, and the number of
predictions that lie inside a declared domain is 1{,}105{,}593{,}143 rather than
the 2.57 billion that asking every task about every crystal would produce, a
factor of 2.3. The difference is not a loss of coverage. It is the removal of numbers
that would have looked like data. Plotting what a task may be asked about against
what it already knows separates the two situations that matter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:completion}c): a task lying far above the diagonal has the most to
gain, because its domain is large and its labels are few, while a task lying to
the right and low is one whose domain is already nearly labelled and where a
model has little left to add.

One dependency in that structure is worth stating because it closes the loop with
Section~\ref{sec:index}. Twenty-one of the tasks are gated on a property the
database does not yet hold: whether a crystal has a gap, or whether it is
magnetically ordered, is unknown for 108{,}246{,}933 structure-task pairs, so
their admissibility can only be decided once the corresponding tier-1 models have
run.
The completion therefore proceeds in stages, and each stage enlarges the domain
over which the next one is defined, in the same way that each admitted structure
enlarged the substrate against which the next database was matched.

What such a completion would settle is worth stating before it is attempted,
because it is the reason the corpus was assembled rather than merely enlarged.
Searching the known set for a material that already has the property wanted does
not require that the property have been computed, since a surrogate model can
rank crystals nobody has evaluated, and that is how such campaigns are run. What
is bounded is the pool the ranking is taken over and the labels the ranking was
learned from, and Section~\ref{sec:inherits} measured both. Running the
completion over the whole corpus, within the domains of
Fig.~\ref{fig:completion}b,c, moves them together: the pool becomes the
deduplicated record rather than one provider's slice of it, and every prediction
carries the spread of the sources that trained it.

That is what measures a quantity the field has not had a way to ask about: how
much of the known record has been passed over for want of a calculation rather
than for want of merit. The answer is consequential in either direction. A large
yield would mean that forward screening has been operating far inside its own
reach, with materials that can already be made waiting to be recognised. A small
one would mean the reach is nearly exhausted, and that further progress has to
come from designing structures rather than from finding them \textemdash{} which
is the route the next section turns to.

\subsection{Judging the novelty claimed by inverse design}
\label{sec:novelty}

The record that bounds forward screening is also the reference inverse design
needs, and the point of this section is that a reported novelty fraction measures
that reference at least as much as it measures the model. A generative model can
only be shown to have produced something new against a reference that is both
large enough to contain what else has been computed and complete enough to
include the databases nobody thinks to check; short of that, the fraction
reported as new is a statement about the omission. How much that matters is
easiest to see in two released sample sets matched in everything but the
generator, both ternary throughout. Under the test the field reports, a language
model trained on a repository snapshot and a random enumeration of compositions
differ by eighteen points. Under the test of whether anybody had computed the
composition before, they differ by a factor of eighty, and in the direction that
says the language model never left the space it was shown. A generative model
counts as new whatever is absent from the reference set it was given, so a
structure missing from one repository but held by another is counted as new; the
reference sets in current use are drawn from a handful of the largest
repositories \cite{ramlaoui2025lemat,betala2025lematgenbench}, and the three
assessments cited in the Introduction all turned on exactly that
\cite{cheetham2024artificial,juelsholt2026continued,szymanski2025baselines}.

Two distinct failures sit behind those three assessments, and the corpus
assembled here has already met both from the other direction. The first is a
criterion too permissive, counting a known prototype under a new decoration as a
new material. The second is a criterion that misses a match it should have made,
which is what happens when an ordered approximant is generated for a parent phase
that is disordered on one site. Section~\ref{sec:index} reports the first as
over-merging in the enumerated framework libraries, where a bonding-graph
fingerprint collapses more than $10^5$ distinct hypothetical frameworks onto a
few hundred pool materials, and Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:fingerprint}
states the domain within which that fingerprint is valid. Both are failures of the criterion rather than of the generator, and no published
novelty figure states which criterion produced it.

What this record offers the route is therefore not a better score but a reference
that can be named, and a criterion that travels with the number. Two groups
auditing the same generator against the resolved corpus, under the same matching
rule and the same stated domain of validity, obtain the same novelty fraction;
against a reference assembled from a few of the largest repositories under an
unstated rule, they need not. That is the condition under which a novelty claim
becomes a measurement rather than a report, and it is what the audit reported
below is built to supply.

The audit fixes the reference and varies the criterion. Nine released structure
sets were put through it (Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}; Methods): the 554{,}056
crystals GNoME contributes to this corpus \cite{merchant2023scaling}; the 770
structures MatterGen publishes with its paper \cite{zeni2025generative}, together
with 256 crystals generated here from its released unconditional checkpoint and
1{,}081 from the checkpoint conditioned on bulk modulus; and the 500-structure
samples released under a common relaxation protocol for CDVAE
\cite{xie2022crystal}, CrystaLLM \cite{antunes2024crystal}, FTCP, ion exchange
and random enumeration \cite{szymanski2025baselines}. The reference is all 55
sources. Four criteria are applied in turn, from strict to permissive: the
crystal itself, under the structural fingerprint of Section~\ref{sec:index}; the
reduced composition together with the space group; the reduced composition alone;
and the stoichiometry pattern together with the space group
(Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}a). The three MatterGen
sets agree to within 1.8 points under every criterion, so the unconditional run
stands for them below and in the figure.

At the strict end the sets are largely new, and widening the reference does not
change that as much as one would expect. Against Materials Project alone every
set is 98--100\% new; against all 55 sources the figures are 96.7\% for GNoME,
95.0\% for CDVAE, 89.4\% for random enumeration, 86.8\% for FTCP, 82.0\% for
MatterGen, 72.0\% for ion exchange and 70.8\% for CrystaLLM
(Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}b). Where the few matches sit is more informative than how
many there are. Pooled over the six sample sets, the databases holding the
structures a strict test still recognises are NOMAD (323), Alexandria (321),
AFLOW (276) and OQMD (165); Materials Project, the reference such claims are most
often taken against, holds 19 (Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}c). For GNoME the asymmetry
is sharper: of its 18{,}234 exact matches, Alexandria alone accounts for 14{,}709
and Materials Project for one, which is what deduplicating against one repository
and not against another looks like from the other side.

Loosening the criterion moves the number far more than widening the reference
does, and it is tempting to conclude that a structure should count as new only if
it clears all four tests, on which reading almost nothing does: 0.0--8.2\% of the
sample sets and 45.1\% of GNoME (Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}d). That conclusion should
be resisted, because the four criteria are not of one kind. The first three are
decidable identity tests: a hit establishes that the record holds this crystal,
or a crystal of this composition in this space group, or a crystal of this
composition. The fourth is not. Sharing a stoichiometry pattern and a space group
is a necessary condition for two crystals to be the same prototype under a
different decoration, not a sufficient one, so a hit establishes only that
prototype identity cannot be excluded cheaply. Requiring a structure to clear it
would call a genuinely new prototype known because some unrelated compound
happens to share its anonymous formula and space group, and among 14.59 million
crystals over 230 space groups almost nothing escapes that. The fourth column is
therefore an upper bound on prototype redundancy rather than a test a claim must
pass; settling it needs a structural comparison the fingerprint does not perform,
and Supplementary Note~\ref{si-note:fingerprint} states what that would involve.

The strictest criterion that can be both decided and defended is the third, and
it is the one a claim should carry: the fraction of a set whose reduced
composition appears nowhere in the record. That fraction is 91.1\% for GNoME,
60.0\% for CDVAE, 34.0\% for MatterGen, 25.8\% for ion exchange, 16.7\% for
random enumeration, 3.0\% for FTCP and 0.2\% for CrystaLLM. Removing GNoME from
the reference, so that one generated set is not used to judge another, moves none
of these by more than 0.4 points. GNoME's 91.1\% is not a discovery rate:
87.4\% of its released set carries four or five elements against 29.2\% of the
record as a whole, and in that part of composition space a composition being
absent carries little information about whether the crystal is distinct from what
is known.

Two of these figures should be read together, because the sets behind them are
matched: the CrystaLLM and random-enumeration samples are ternary throughout.
Under the fingerprint the two look like a matter of degree, 70.8\% against
89.4\% absent from the record. At the composition level they are two orders of
magnitude apart: 499 of CrystaLLM's 500 structures have a composition the record
already holds, against 410 of random enumeration's 492. A language model trained
on a repository snapshot rearranges compositions it was shown; random enumeration
leaves that space. Neither behaviour is visible in the number that is usually
reported, and a leaderboard ordered by that number places the two within twenty
points of each other.

What the audit supports is therefore not a ranking of generators but a reporting
convention. A novelty fraction is a property of three things, and only one of
them is the model: the structures, the reference they were checked against, and
the criterion that decided a match. Both of the other two are cheap to state and
neither is usually stated. The resolved record supplies a reference that can be
named and held fixed; the ladder supplies criteria that can be named and applied
in turn; and reporting the fraction under each, rather than one figure under an
unstated rule, is what turns a novelty claim into a measurement that a second
group can reproduce. The requirement this places on the reference is the one this
work was built to meet. It is not that the reference be a repository of record,
but that it be large enough that the relevant prior computation is in it at all,
and complete enough that the aggregating and specialised databases which turn out
to hold most of the matches are not the ones left out.

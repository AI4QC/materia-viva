\section{Introduction}

Materials discovery has become an exercise in reusing the collective published
record. Public repositories now hold millions of computed crystals
\cite{jain2013commentary,saal2013materials,curtarolo2012aflow,schmidt2022dataset},
generative models propose new ones directly
\cite{xie2022crystal,zeni2025generative,merchant2023scaling}, and the universal
interatomic potentials used to filter those proposals are trained on whatever the
community has already published
\cite{chen2022universal,deng2023chgnet,batatia2023foundation,yang2024mattersim,wood2025uma}.
Crystal structures, in other words, are not scarce.

What is scarce is a materials space that can be searched, compared and traced
back to its source in one place. In current practice a screening campaign begins
by requesting accounts one repository at a time, calling bulk interfaces,
downloading archives in incompatible formats, and then working out which of the
resulting records are genuinely distinct crystals rather than duplicates of one
another, intermediate relaxation frames or frames of a molecular-dynamics
trajectory. Property tables arrive detached from the structures they describe,
units and conventions differ between providers, and the licence position of each
source has to be established again before anything can be redistributed. The data
already exists; a large share of the effort goes into acquiring and tidying it
before any candidate is screened.

The reason this preparation cannot be done once and shared is that the
repositories agree on no key at the level of the object they describe. The same
arrangement of atoms carries a different accession number in every database that
holds it, and the one property they genuinely share, the chemical formula, cannot
serve as a key, because polymorphs share a formula and differ in almost
everything a screening campaign cares about. Without a key at the level of the
crystal, the union cannot be deduplicated, values from different providers cannot
be brought onto the same object, and three quantities that bear directly on how
materials data is used stay unmeasured: how much of the corpus is the same
crystal counted repeatedly, whether two groups that computed the same quantity
for the same crystal obtained the same answer, and how the properties a discovery
campaign actually needs are distributed over the structures that exist.

Assembling such a union once would in any case not be enough. Every repository
publishes new releases, and new repositories appear faster than any is retired,
so a union built in a single pass is out of date before it is finished and has to
be rebuilt from nothing to absorb what arrived while it was being made. A corpus
that is to stay current has to do something a merge does not: recognise material
it has never seen, and fold it in without discarding the ability to recognise
that it has already acquired.

Substantial progress has been made on the parts of this problem that can be
solved one layer at a time. The OPTIMADE specification gave more than twenty
providers a common query interface, removing the burden of a separate account and
format for each \cite{andersen2021optimade,evans2024developments}. Structural
fingerprints made deduplication tractable at scale, and the LeMaterial initiative
used one to publish a deduplicated union of several large repositories
\cite{ramlaoui2025lemat}. What neither delivers is the property layer, which is
where the remaining difficulty sits. Knowing that two records describe the same
crystal does not attach what each source says about it, and attaching those
statements is useless unless they stay apart afterwards. Two repositories
publishing a column labelled band gap are frequently not reporting the same
physical quantity: a semilocal, a hybrid and a measured value differ by more than
the differences a screening campaign is trying to resolve, so a union built by
matching column names would average them and destroy exactly the information that
makes the corpus worth consulting.

Here we build that space. Identity is resolved across 54 databases at the level
of the crystal structure, and every resulting property value retains the source,
the native record and the calculation or measurement that produced it, so that
several sources speaking about one crystal remain separately audible. Licence
boundaries are enforced in the pipeline rather than by convention, so that the
publishable corpus and the locally buildable one are distinct by construction.
And admission and recognition are made the same operation: a record that matches
nothing is not an error to be logged but the corpus's next member, and the moment
it is admitted its structural fingerprint joins the criterion against which every
subsequent record is judged. The database therefore evolves as it ingests, and a
newly released version or an entirely new repository is folded into a substrate
that has grown since the last one rather than matched against a frozen reference.

The space so built answers the three questions its absence had left open, and the
answers are not what a user of any single repository would assume. Redundancy is
large and strongly concentrated. Independent sources reporting the same quantity
for the same crystal disagree by margins exceeding the thresholds on which
materials selection routinely turns, and a substantial part of their apparent
agreement is shared provenance rather than independent confirmation. Coverage of
the property space is governed by what a property costs to compute rather than by
what it is worth knowing, which turns the remaining gap into a learning problem
the corpus is itself equipped to pose. The instrument and the corpus it produces
are described in Methods and in the Supplementary Information; what follows is
what the corpus turns out to contain.
